Check Event Agency References Against Your RFP Scope

A reference call can confirm that an agency is pleasant to work with without telling you whether it can run your event. The customer may have bought a different package, worked with a different producer or relied on a larger internal team. Ask for references after you understand what the agency is proposing. Then use the call to test specific assumptions about the work, the people and the handoffs you would buy.

Ask for examples that share your constraints

Give the agency a short description of the event and the parts it would own. If you need it to coordinate speakers, guest arrivals and a private discussion, ask for a reference who used the agency for those tasks. A much larger event can still be relevant if the delivery dependencies were similar. The executive event planning guide can help your team describe the important constraints before choosing which examples to ask about.

Ask which agency roles the reference worked with and whether those roles are included in your proposal. A customer who had a dedicated account lead may have had a different experience from one who used a shared service desk. If the reference worked with people who would also work on your event, ask what those people actually owned. Use the answer to clarify the scope and availability your team can expect.

Turn proposal assumptions into specific questions

Take the event management RFP template and mark the requirements with the most uncertainty. For each, write a question about a real action the reference could describe. Instead of asking whether the agency communicated well, ask how the client learned a venue deadline had changed and what happened next. A precise question gives the reference something they can recall and gives reviewers a basis for comparing answers.

Use the same core questions with references for each finalist. It is fine to add a few questions about differences in the proposal. Note what the reference saw directly and where they are passing along an impression from someone else. If they did not work on reporting, for example, ask who received the files or leave that item for the agency to demonstrate. A missing answer is a prompt to check the evidence elsewhere.

Look for evidence that the agency understood the audience

Ask how the agency responded when the guest list or program changed. A room of buyers exploring a new problem may need a different discussion from a room of people evaluating specific solutions. This guide to B2B event formats and buying stages describes how the audience affects the format and follow-up. A relevant reference can tell you whether the agency noticed that difference and which practical changes it made.

For instance, a reference might describe replacing a presentation with a shorter opening and more time for questions. Ask who suggested the change, who approved it and how the change reached the venue and moderator. If the agency helped with follow-up, ask what the account team received and when. The important detail is whether the agency connected decisions about the room to the work people needed to do after it ended.

Bring the findings back to the scope before signing

Record each answer next to the assumption it supports or calls into question. Keep the summary brief and avoid including confidential details from the previous customer. If a reference says the agency turned around a revised schedule within a day, ask the bidder whether the same response window is included for your event. If the response depended on a premium service, ask for a price and include it in the comparison where relevant.

Use the call to identify where a written commitment would help. For example, add a named replacement for the lead producer, a delivery deadline for the attendance file or a defined escalation path for venue changes. Ask the bidder to review those points with the people who would deliver the work. An encouraging reference can guide that conversation, but the resulting commitments should be visible in the agreement.

Once the work is awarded, share the agreed changes with your internal delivery lead. If a reference exposed a gap your team will fill, confirm who owns that task and when it starts. The call pays off when the people planning the event can see which decisions were tested and what they need to do with the result.
